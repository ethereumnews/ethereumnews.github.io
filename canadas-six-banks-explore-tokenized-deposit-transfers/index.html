Canada’s six banks explore tokenized deposit transfers

Six Canadian banks will explore interbank transfers of tokenized Canadian-dollar deposits to support faster, programmable payments and future digital-asset links.

The Web3 Hub DeskSep 24, 20262 min read

Six Canadian banks said on Sept. 22 they are jointly exploring Canadian-dollar tokenized deposits, starting with a project to move them between financial institutions. A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit represented digitally; Canada’s banking regulator says it is not legally distinct from a traditional deposit. Think of it as changing the form of a bank’s record of money, while the deposit remains a deposit.

The participants are Bank of Montreal, Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce, National Bank of Canada, Royal Bank of Canada, The Bank of Nova Scotia and TD Bank Group. Their jointly announced initiative is an exploration, not a live payment service. The banks say the first phase aims to move tokenized deposits efficiently across Canadian institutions, with a longer-term goal of connecting to other digital-asset initiatives.

What will the banks test first?

The initial focus is transfers of tokenized deposits between participating financial institutions. The banks have not said how those transfers will be built or settled: their announcement names no blockchain, shared ledger, launch date, transaction volume or customer access plan. It also does not say that customers can use tokenized deposits now.

The group says it is seeking faster, more efficient and programmable payments while maintaining safety, stability and regulatory oversight. Programmability can allow payment instructions to be tied to conditions, but the banks have not described any specific use case or payment feature for this project. They say other deposit-taking institutions could join “at the appropriate time.”

What does the regulator say about tokenized deposits?

The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions said on Sept. 10 that a deposit’s underlying technology does not determine its legal nature: tokenized deposits are “not legally distinct from traditional deposits.” The regulator also says banks remain responsible for complying with applicable laws and guidance, and are expected to consult their OSFI lead supervisors before launching novel products or services. That clarification sets out how the regulator views the product; it is not an approval of this particular initiative.

The six-bank project follows that clarification, but its announcement gives no implementation timetable or technical design. The banks say they want to connect the system over time to other digital-asset initiatives; they have not named those projects. The first step, if the exploration proceeds, is institutional transfers of digitally represented deposits—not a new retail coin or a service customers can sign up for today. For readers, the concrete change is a joint effort to investigate bank-to-bank tokenized payments. Whether it becomes a working network, and when, remains unknown. OSFI’s statement and The Block’s report provide additional context.
