Bridge routes trade speed for settlement certainty

Bridge routes can deliver tokens sooner through liquidity providers, while canonical exits follow the source chain’s settlement rules and may take longer.

By Web3 Hub NewsroomSep 30, 20262 min read

A bridge user chooses between a faster route that fronts destination tokens and a canonical exit that waits for the source chain’s settlement rules. The faster option can save time, but it adds a provider and a later settlement step; the canonical route usually follows the protocol’s own withdrawal process.

How does a bridge route move tokens?

A bridge route coordinates actions on two blockchains, which do not share a single transaction ledger. Depending on the bridge design, tokens may be locked on the source chain and released on the destination, burned and minted across chains, or supplied from a pool of destination-chain liquidity.

Some interfaces compare routes from multiple bridges or liquidity providers, so the same source and destination can produce different fees, estimated times and settlement paths. For more on how Bungee bridge routes handle transfers and delays, see the fuller explanation; the key distinction is whether the destination funds are already available or must wait for a source-chain withdrawal.

Why can a fast bridge route arrive sooner?

A liquidity provider can send destination tokens before the source chain’s canonical process has finished. The user gets funds sooner, while the provider later claims or settles against the source-side transaction. This is a timing service: it does not make the source chain finalize faster.

The quoted arrival time is an estimate, not a guarantee. It can depend on whether a provider has enough liquidity, whether the source transaction confirms, and whether the destination chain is congested. The route may charge for that convenience through a fee, a less favorable exchange rate, or both.

Compare routes using the amount expected to arrive, not just the headline fee or fastest estimate. A useful check is to review:

  • The destination amount after fees and any token conversion.
  • Whether a provider is advancing liquidity or the route waits for a protocol withdrawal.
  • What happens if the source transaction fails or the route times out.
  • Whether the destination token is the one you intend to hold or use.

When should you use the canonical exit?

Use the canonical exit when following the protocol’s native withdrawal path matters more than receiving funds quickly. It typically relies on the source chain’s bridge contracts and settlement conditions; for some systems, those conditions include a waiting period before funds can be claimed.

That waiting period is not necessarily a sign that a transfer is stuck. It may be part of the security model: the protocol allows time for its settlement rules to complete before releasing funds. Check the bridge’s status and instructions for the relevant network pair, since steps and timing vary by protocol.

For a small transfer needed immediately, a faster route may be worth its extra cost if its provider and final amount are clear. For a larger transfer, or one where you can wait, the canonical path is often the simpler choice because it avoids relying on a liquidity provider’s advance. In either case, confirm the source network, destination network and token before sending; once the source transaction is final, a wrong destination choice may be difficult to reverse.
