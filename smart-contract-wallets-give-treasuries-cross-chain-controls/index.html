Smart contract wallets give treasuries cross-chain controls

Smart contract wallets let treasury teams route approvals and transfers across chains, but bridge choice, execution controls and arrival checks determine how much control they keep.

By Web3 Hub NewsroomOct 17, 2026 · 09:34 UTC3 min read

Smart contract wallets let treasury teams set approval rules for transfers across blockchains, while a bridge or messaging service moves funds between them. The wallet can control who approves a payment and what actions an authorised transaction may perform; it does not make separate chains share one balance.

A transfer usually has two stages: a transaction on the source chain and a corresponding delivery or action on the destination chain. For the steps involved in sending through a relay bridge and verifying arrival, use a guide focused on that process.

How do smart contract wallets approve cross-chain transfers?

The wallet checks a proposed transaction against rules written into its contract. A treasury can require several approvers, restrict which addresses may receive funds, or limit what an operator’s key can do. The exact controls depend on the wallet’s implementation.

That approval governs the transaction submitted to the wallet. The bridge or messaging service handles the cross-chain leg under its own design, so treasury policy should cover both the source transaction and the destination address or action.

What happens during a cross-chain transfer?

The source-chain transaction locks, burns or otherwise commits the assets, depending on the bridge’s mechanism. The service then relays a message or releases a corresponding asset on the destination chain. These steps are separate, so arrival may take time and a transfer can require follow-up if the destination action does not complete.

Before approving, treasury operators should check:

  • The source and destination networks.
  • The destination address and token.
  • The amount expected after any fees.
  • Whether the recipient can use the asset delivered on that chain.

A relay bridge may use an outside relayer to submit or coordinate part of the transfer. That can simplify execution, but it adds a service and its operating assumptions to the transfer path. A bridge’s name alone does not establish that two assets are interchangeable or that a transfer has completed.

How should a treasury verify arrival?

Check the destination chain for the transaction and confirm the receiving address, token and amount. Treat a source-chain confirmation as evidence that the first leg occurred, not proof that the destination balance is usable. The wallet interface can help, but verify against destination-chain records when the amount or timing matters.

For routine payments, the better setup is usually one that makes approvals, permitted destinations and post-transfer checks clear to multiple operators. Keep a record linking the approved request to both chain transactions; reconcile the destination balance before marking the payment complete.
